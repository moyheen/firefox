The best argument against the tyranny of urgency is, paradoxically, that very few things are actually urgent. We have built our working lives around the assumption that everything is a fire, every message is a request that must be answered now, every meeting must produce a decision before the hour is up. And yet, when we look back at the work we have done over the course of a year, almost none of it required the speed that we forced on it.

Consider the simple act of reading. To read well is to read slowly, to let an idea settle, to question it, to return to a sentence and notice what we missed the first time. None of this is compatible with the steady drumbeat of notifications. We treat reading as something that happens between other things, a snack rather than a meal. We are surprised when our thinking feels shallow, when our conversations feel rushed, when our judgments feel uncertain. But these are the natural consequences of feeding the mind in scraps.

Slowness is not the absence of work. It is a different relationship to work. The person who reads one book carefully has often learned more than the person who has skimmed a dozen. The team that takes a week to decide carefully often saves a month of cleanup later. There is a discipline to going slow that is harder than going fast, and it is the discipline we have been steadily losing.
